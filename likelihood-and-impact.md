# Likelihood and Impact Scoring

Likelihood and impact are the two fundamental dimensions of a basic risk scoring model.

A risk score is commonly calculated as:

**Risk Score = Likelihood × Impact**

Consistent definitions are important because different assessors may otherwise assign very different scores to similar risks.

## Likelihood

Likelihood represents the probability or expected frequency of a risk occurring.

A basic five-level scale can be used.

| Score | Rating         | General Description                       |
| ----: | -------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
|     1 | Rare           | Highly unlikely to occur                  |
|     2 | Unlikely       | Could occur but is not expected           |
|     3 | Possible       | Could occur under certain circumstances   |
|     4 | Likely         | Expected to occur in some circumstances   |
|     5 | Almost Certain | Expected to occur frequently or very soon |

Organizations may define these categories using historical data, frequency, probability ranges, expert judgement, or other criteria.

## Impact

Impact represents the severity of consequences if a risk occurs.

| Score | Rating        | General Description                              |
| ----: | ------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
|     1 | Insignificant | Minimal effect on objectives                     |
|     2 | Minor         | Limited effect                                   |
|     3 | Moderate      | Noticeable effect requiring management attention |
|     4 | Major         | Significant effect on objectives                 |
|     5 | Severe        | Serious or potentially critical consequences     |

## Why Definitions Matter

Consider two people assessing the same risk.

Person A may consider an event "Likely" because it happened once last year.

Person B may consider it "Possible" because similar events have historically happened only once every several years.

Without agreed definitions, their scores may differ significantly.

A documented scoring methodology improves consistency.

## Consider Multiple Impact Dimensions

Depending on the organization, impact may need to consider:

* Financial
* Operational
* Regulatory
* Legal
* Customer
* Reputation
* Security
* Privacy
* Safety
* Strategic objectives

Some organizations use the highest impact across these dimensions, while others use a more complex calculation.

## Example

Suppose an organization assesses a supplier disruption.

Likelihood:

`3 – Possible`

Impact:

`4 – Major`

Risk score:

`3 × 4 = 12`

The organization can then compare the result against its risk-rating thresholds.

## Avoid False Precision

A risk score should support decision-making rather than create a false impression of mathematical certainty.

For example, a score of 12 does not necessarily mean that a risk is exactly twice as serious as a risk with a score of 6.

The score is a structured indicator that helps prioritize attention.

## Document Your Methodology

A risk methodology should explain:

* What each likelihood score means
* What each impact score means
* How scores are calculated
* How risk ratings are determined
* Who can approve risk acceptance
* How exceptions are handled
* How assessments are reviewed
